Evidence-Based Tools for Learning Math Effectively

Welcome! This workshop gives you practical, research-backed strategies for succeeding in math courses. You'll learn three study techniques that consistently work, how to use tutoring effectively, and how AI tools can support your learning.

What to Expect from Tutoring

Let's start with realistic expectations. Research shows that effective tutoring typically improves student outcomes by about 0.4 to 0.8 standard deviations. In practical terms, this means:

  • Moving from a C to a B grade (or from failing to passing)
  • Improving test scores by 15-25 percentage points
  • Going from the 50th percentile to approximately the 70th percentile

These aren't miracles, but they're meaningful, achievable improvements. The key is consistency. Research on high-dosage tutoring shows that 3+ sessions per week produces dramatically better results than once-weekly sessions.

Bottom line: Regular tutoring works. Students who use the Student Learning Center (SLC) consistently have approximately 20% higher pass rates than those who don't.

Three Study Strategies That Actually Work

Most students study ineffectively. Research shows the most popular strategies—highlighting, rereading, and cramming—have the weakest evidence. Three techniques have the strongest research support:

1. Retrieval Practice (Self-Testing)

Effect size: 0.70 (equivalent to moving from a C to a B)

Retrieval practice means actively recalling information rather than passively reviewing it. When you reread notes, material feels familiar—but familiarity doesn't equal learning. Struggling to retrieve information strengthens memory far more than effortless review.

How to use it:

  • Do practice problems without looking at solutions first
  • Spend 20-30 minutes genuinely attempting a problem before seeking help
  • Use flashcards or quiz yourself on concepts
  • Close your notes and try to explain a concept from memory

2. Spaced Practice (Distributed Practice)

Effect size: 0.47-0.60 (equivalent to half a letter grade improvement)

Spacing means distributing practice over time rather than cramming in one session. Studying 30 minutes per day for six days beats studying 3 hours in one marathon session—even though total time is the same.

Research shows spacing increases math test scores by one-third to one-half of a letter grade. Critically, across-semester retention benefits exclusively from increased spacing, not from increased practice amount.

How to use it:

  • Study a little bit every day instead of long sessions right before exams
  • Review material from previous weeks, not just current material
  • Start test preparation 7-10 days in advance, not 1-2 days
  • The optimal gap between sessions: 10-20% of how long you need to remember it

3. Interleaved Practice

Effect size: 0.83 (the highest of any study strategy)

Interleaving means mixing different problem types during practice rather than blocking them by topic. Most textbooks block problems: 20 quadratic formula problems, then 20 factoring problems, then 20 completing-the-square problems.

Research shows interleaved practice yields 61% performance versus 38% for blocked practice. The mechanism is crucial: interleaving forces you to discriminate between problem types and choose appropriate strategies, rather than mindlessly applying the same procedure repeatedly.

How to use it:

  • Instead of working straight through one textbook section, mix Chapter 1, 2, and 3 problems together
  • Create mixed practice sets that require you to identify what type of problem it is
  • When reviewing for exams, shuffle different problem types rather than organizing by topic

Why Math Matters: Building a Valuable Brain

You might be thinking: "When will I ever use this? I'll always have a calculator." That's the wrong question. Learning math isn't about memorizing formulas for situations you might never encounter—it's about developing a mind that can think through complex problems.

Math Is a Skeleton Key

Mathematical concepts are uniquely portable across every human endeavor. The same slope concept that describes business revenue growth is used to plot flight paths in aviation. The mean your professor uses to calculate your grade is the same tool casinos use to estimate profitability and hospitals use to track patient outcomes. Understanding rates of change helps financial analysts predict market trends, epidemiologists model disease spread, and engineers optimize fuel efficiency.

When you learn mathematics, you're not just learning isolated skills—you're acquiring a universal language that unlocks understanding across domains. From healthcare to electrical engineering, from economics to climate science, the same mathematical patterns appear again and again. Recognizing these patterns gives you access to fields and conversations that would otherwise remain closed.

Understanding Patterns Is How You Build Value

Surface-level knowledge—knowing what to do—can be replaced by technology. Deeper understanding—knowing why it works and when to apply it—cannot. When you learn to see the mathematical structure underlying a problem, you develop transferable problem-solving ability that applies far beyond any specific formula.

This is why struggling with challenging math problems matters, even if you'll never face that exact problem again. Athletes don't lift weights because they expect to pick up heavy objects in daily life—they lift weights because resistance training builds strength that applies to everything they do. Mathematics is resistance training for knowledge workers. Every difficult problem you work through strengthens your ability to break down complexity, identify patterns, test hypotheses, and reason systematically.

The Real Goal: A Mind That Can Think

The ultimate purpose of mathematical education isn't to turn you into a human calculator. It's to develop a brain that can:

  • Recognize when a problem has underlying structure that can be analyzed systematically
  • Break complex situations into manageable components
  • Identify what information you need versus what you already know
  • Test whether a solution makes sense before accepting it
  • Transfer problem-solving approaches from familiar contexts to unfamiliar ones

These are the skills that make you valuable in any career, any situation, any challenge you face. The specific mathematics you're learning today is the vehicle—the capacity to think clearly under complexity is the destination.

So when you're struggling with a difficult concept, remember: you're not just learning math. You're building a mind capable of solving problems that don't exist yet, in fields that haven't been invented, using tools nobody has created. That's why it matters.

Using AI Tools Effectively

AI tutoring has reached genuine effectiveness—research shows AI tutors designed with pedagogical guardrails perform comparably to human tutoring. The key phrase is "designed with pedagogical guardrails."

Use AI Tutor Pro: No Download or Login Required

AI Tutor Pro is a free, ChatGPT-powered tool specifically designed for learning. Unlike asking ChatGPT directly for answers, AI Tutor Pro uses Socratic questioning—it guides you to find answers yourself through strategic questions.

Best practices for AI tutoring:

  • Use it for practice between human tutoring sessions (Tuesday/Thursday at SLC, Monday/Wednesday/Friday/Sunday with AI)
  • Ask it to guide you, not give you answers: "Help me understand how to approach this" not "What's the answer?"
  • Explain your thinking: "I think I should use the quadratic formula here because... Is that right?"
  • Use it when you're stuck: AI has infinite patience for the 2am problem-solving session
  • Check your work: "I got x = 5. Can you check my reasoning?"

Where AI Tutoring Goes Wrong (And How to Prevent It)

Warning: A 2025 study found that students using unrestricted ChatGPT improved practice performance by 48% but underperformed when the AI was removed—a "crutch effect" that undermines skill development.

How to avoid the crutch effect:

  • Never use AI on graded homework unless explicitly permitted by your instructor
  • Test yourself without AI regularly to ensure you can perform independently
  • Use AI for understanding, not answers—if you're just copying solutions, you're not learning
  • Combine AI with human tutoring—AI for practice, humans for judgment and complex concepts
  • Ask AI to explain its reasoning rather than just accepting solutions

Think of AI tools as practice infrastructure, not replacements for learning. They're available 24/7 when human tutors aren't, but they work best when integrated with regular human tutoring at the SLC.

Common Questions and Concerns

I'm not good at math. Can tutoring really help?

Yes. Mathematical ability grows with practice and effective strategy. Research on growth mindset shows that believing you can improve is itself predictive of improvement. The strategies in this workshop—retrieval practice, spacing, and interleaving—work regardless of your current skill level.

I don't have time for 3+ tutoring sessions per week.

This is where AI tools become valuable. You can't always make it to the SLC, but AI Tutor Pro is available 24/7. Even 2 SLC sessions + 3 AI practice sessions produces better outcomes than 1 SLC session alone. Do what you can with the time you have.

Won't using AI make me dependent on it?

Only if you use it incorrectly. Using AI to get answers creates dependency. Using AI to practice explaining your reasoning, to get hints when stuck, and to check your work builds independence. The key is testing yourself regularly without AI to ensure you can perform independently.

I've tried tutoring before and it didn't help.

Tutoring effectiveness depends on dosage and approach. Once-weekly tutoring shows weak evidence. Three-plus times weekly shows strong evidence. Also consider: Were you using effective study strategies between sessions? Research shows students need both expert instruction AND effective practice strategies.

How do I know if I'm really learning or just getting better at practice problems?

This is called "metacognitive awareness"—thinking about your thinking. Good signs you're actually learning: You can explain concepts to someone else, you can solve problems without referring to examples, you recognize when to use different strategies. Bad signs: You can only solve problems that look exactly like examples, you can't explain why a procedure works, you freeze when problem formats change slightly.

What if I fail anyway?

First, using these strategies significantly reduces that probability. Second, if you do struggle, you'll have built relationships with SLC tutors who can help you understand what went wrong and plan differently for next time. Third, struggling with material while using effective strategies still builds understanding—you'll be better prepared when you encounter this material again.
